### **Clarity isn't certainty**

We expect a lot from leaders - we expect them to know where the
organisation is going, understand what is happening around them and make
good decisions about what happens next.

Perhaps, more than anything, we expect them to have answers. That
expectation might have made more sense when organisations operated in
relatively predictable environments, but I'm increasingly unconvinced it
works in the environments many leaders find themselves in today.

Markets change. Technology changes. Customers behave in unexpected ways.
Competitors emerge from places we weren't looking. AI is changing
capabilities faster than most organisations can understand what to do
with them.

And increasingly, decisions have to be made before all of the
information is available.

The uncomfortable reality is that sometimes **the leader doesn't know
either** but... I don't think that's necessarily a failure of
leadership. Pretending otherwise, however, just might be.

There is an important difference between **certainty and clarity**.

Certainty says:

> ***"I know what is going to happen."***

Clarity says:

> ***"We don't know exactly what is going to happen, but we understand
> what we're trying to achieve, what matters most and how we're going to
> make decisions as we learn."***

Those are very different things. Perhaps one of the most important
responsibilities of leadership in a complex organisation isn't creating
certainty at all.

It's creating enough clarity for people to act without it.

## **When Clarity is Missing**

The absence of clarity rarely announces itself as a leadership problem.
Typically, it appears somewhere else in the organisation and gets given
a different name.

Decisions take too long; people seem reluctant to take ownership and
teams become frustrated with one another. Meetings multiply, priorities
compete and relatively straightforward questions find their way
increasingly high up the organisation before somebody feels able to
answer them.

We might describe this as bureaucracy, poor accountability, resistance
to change or perhaps even a problem with organisational culture.

Sometimes this is the case, but I wonder how often we're looking at the
symptoms rather than the cause.

If people don't know what matters most, everything can become a
priority. If they don't understand where their authority begins and
ends, escalation becomes the safest option.

If people don't understand why a decision has been made, they may follow
it while the circumstances remain exactly as expected, but struggle to
adapt when circumstances change.

Perhaps most damagingly, people start learning that making a decision
themselves carries more risk than asking somebody else to make it.

Over time, that behaviour can become self-reinforcing. Leaders become
frustrated that people aren't taking ownership, so they become more
involved. As leaders become more involved, people become increasingly
accustomed to looking upwards for answers.

> ***Decisions move further away from the people closest to the problem
> and the organisation becomes slower, despite everybody apparently
> working harder to make it faster.***

This can create an uncomfortable contradiction. An organisation can talk
extensively about empowerment while simultaneously creating an
environment in which people don't feel particularly empowered at all.

Telling somebody they have ownership isn't especially useful if they
don't know what they're allowed to decide, which trade-offs they're
permitted to make or whether they'll be supported when a reasonable
decision turns out to be wrong.

The same problem appears during change. What looks like resistance isn't
always resistance to the change itself.

People may understand what they're being asked to do without
understanding why they're being asked to do it, what problem it is
supposed to solve or what should take priority when the new expectation
conflicts with everything they were already doing.

In those circumstances, more communication doesn't necessarily create
more clarity. Organisations can communicate constantly and still leave
people uncertain about what any of it means for the decisions they make
tomorrow morning.

I think clarity has to mean more than communicating a strategy or
explaining a decision. It has to give people enough context to interpret
situations the leader couldn't anticipate.

Otherwise, the organisation may appear aligned while everything is going
according to plan, but the moment something unexpected happens,
everybody looks upwards again.

In a complex organisation, something unexpected is always going to
happen.

## **Clarity Without Control**

The answer isn't for leaders to become more involved. Doing so risks
creating exactly the dependency we're trying to avoid.

Creating clarity doesn't mean telling people what to do in every
situation. As a leader you still have to allow for autonomy of thought
and responsibility -- it should be a key part of the creative process.
It's often how better decisions emerge and ultimately leads to better
results.

It simply means giving them enough context to work it out for
themselves: what are we trying to achieve, what matters most, where does
my authority begin and end, and what trade-offs am I allowed to make?

There will always be decisions that need escalating, but escalation
should be a conscious choice rather than the default response to
uncertainty.

> ***If people understand the purpose, priorities and boundaries,
> decisions can stay closer to the people who understand the problem.***

That also means accepting that people won't always make the decision you
would have made. Empowerment means very little if people are only
empowered when they get it right.

Perhaps that's the distinction between clarity and control:

> ***Control tells people what to do.***
>
> ***Clarity gives them enough understanding to decide what to do.***

**Leaders don't need to have all the answers. Their responsibility is to
create the conditions in which other people can find them.**
