## Part One: The £50 Problem

Four months after being made redundant, I received an email from my former employer asking me to resolve an outstanding expense of approximately £50.

At first, I assumed it was a mistake; perhaps an old distribution list hadn't been updated, or an automated system was still sending notifications to an account that should no longer exist.

But that wasn't quite what had happened - the email had been sent to my personal email address, which hadn't previously been included in the correspondence. Someone appeared to have deliberately added me to an internal financial process, despite the fact that I had left the organisation four months earlier.

The outstanding transaction appeared to relate to a software tool I had previously managed but there was just one rather obvious problem...

**I no longer worked there.**

My position had been made redundant - access to the organisation's systems had been removed and my responsibilities should have been redistributed, and whatever arrangements were necessary to maintain the systems and services I previously managed should have been established as part of that transition.

Yet here I was, four months later, apparently being asked to resolve an administrative issue through a system I could no longer access.

Naturally I found myself wondering what, precisely, the organisation expected me to do about it...

Was I supposed to log into a system for which I no longer had credentials? 

Contact the finance department on behalf of a company that no longer employed me? 

Or perhaps simply accept that, despite being made redundant, I remained somehow responsible for the administrative consequences of my former role?

The amount involved was almost comically insignificant - about £50... yet the organisation had apparently decided that the appropriate response was to involve a former employee rather than resolve the matter internally.

It would be easy to dismiss this as a minor administrative oversight. After all, organisations make mistakes, and £50 is hardly a significant sum for a multinational, multi-billion dollar business is it...?

But I think that misses something rather important.

The interesting question isn't why the organisation was chasing £50, it's why the organisation still appeared to consider someone it had made redundant responsible for resolving it.

This distinction matters because this isn't fundamentally a story about expenses, software subscriptions or administrative incompetence. It's about what happens when organisations confuse removing a position with removing the work that position performed.

And it raises a much more uncomfortable question...

> ***If an organisation decides a role is no longer necessary, but its processes continue to depend on the person who occupied that role, how well did it actually understand the work being done?***
