The old leadership model rewards certainty: expertise, confidence, direction and answers. 

As organisational complexity increases, the probability that one person genuinely understands the whole system decreases.

So leadership becomes less about providing answers and more about **creating the conditions in which good answers can emerge**; asking better questions, bringing different perspectives together, making uncertainty discussable, creating clarity around decisions and knowing when _not_ to intervene.

## The Authority Paradox

Organisations still tend to associate leadership with certainty.

The more senior someone becomes, the greater the expectation that they will know what to do. 

Difficult decisions are escalated upwards, problems are taken to leaders for resolution and confidence is often interpreted as competence.

There is a problem with this model... as decisions move upwards through an organisation, they often move further away from the people who understand the problem best.

The person with the greatest authority to make a decision may have the least direct contact with the customer, the technology, the process or the circumstances in which that decision will actually play out.

Meanwhile, the people closest to the problem can become reluctant to exercise their own judgement - they learn to escalate or look to the leader for the answer. **Eventually, making a decision becomes riskier than not making one.**

This isn't necessarily because they lack the capability to make decisions, but because the organisation has taught them that important answers are supposed to come from somewhere or someone else.

Over time, this creates dependency and leaders become understandably overwhelmed by decisions that shouldn't require their involvement, while capable people further down the organisation become increasingly hesitant to act without permission.

> _**The organisation has concentrated authority at precisely the point where knowledge is becoming more distributed.**_

Complex organisations need something different.

A leader doesn't need to understand every part of the system; they need to understand enough of it to recognise **where the knowledge exists, whose perspective is missing and which questions need to be asked.**

Sometimes the most useful thing a leader can say is:

> _**I don't know. Who does?**_

That isn't an abdication of leadership. It is simply an acknowledgement that expertise is distributed throughout the organisation and that good decisions are more likely to emerge when the right people are able to contribute to them.

The leader still has a critical role - they create clarity around the problem, establish boundaries and priorities. 

They bring together perspectives that might otherwise remain separated. They challenge assumptions. They make disagreement safe. And, when a decision genuinely requires their judgement, they make it.

The difference is that leadership stops being about being **the source of every answer**; it becomes about building an organisation capable of finding better ones.

## When Answers Become Cheap

AI makes this shift even more important.

Throughout organisational history, access to information and expertise was constrained. Leaders often knew more because information flowed upwards. 

Experience accumulated over time, specialist knowledge was difficult to access and analysis could take days or weeks.

That gave knowledge itself considerable value but the introduction of AI has changed that equation.

Information can increasingly be searched, analysed and synthesised in seconds. 

People throughout an organisation can interrogate data, explore unfamiliar subjects, challenge assumptions and generate possible solutions without waiting for expertise to travel through the hierarchy.

Answers are becoming abundant but an abundance of answers doesn't necessarily produce better decisions.

AI can generate ten plausible responses to a problem but it cannot remove the need to decide which one makes sense in the context of the organisation, its customers, its people and the consequences of getting it wrong.

If anything, the proliferation of possible answers makes judgement more important.

> _**When answers become cheap, knowing which questions to ask becomes more valuable.**_

That changes something fundamental about leadership.

The advantage of the leader can no longer simply be that they know more than everyone else. 

The value in a leader lies in their ability to create the conditions in which the organisation can use what it knows.

That means encouraging challenge rather than rewarding agreement and it means bringing together people who see the problem differently.

Leaders need to be clear about which decisions need senior involvement and which should be made closer to the problem.

It means recognising that saying _“I don't know”_ can sometimes be the beginning of good leadership rather than an admission of its failure.

This does not remove accountability - there will still be moments when a leader has incomplete information, competing perspectives and no obviously correct answer. Someone still has to decide.

Is this where leadership matters most?

Not in having all the answers.

But in **knowing when to ask, when to listen, when to trust and when to decide.**
