## Part 1: The Question That Should Have Been Easy

Sometimes the most expensive organisational mistakes begin with a question nobody wants to answer...

Not a particularly difficult question, not even one that challenges the decision itself. A simple request to understand how or when something happened.

Yet rather than provide a straightforward explanation, organisations sometimes respond with carefully constructed justifications, procedural language and reassurances that everything was handled appropriately.

The problem is that justifying a decision is not the same as explaining how it was reached.

When someone asks when a decision was made, explaining why it was necessary doesn't answer the question and when that distinction is repeatedly overlooked, something relatively insignificant begins to grow.

What might have taken five minutes to clarify becomes weeks of correspondence. More people inevitably become involved and trust begins to erode... the original question remains unanswered.

> ***The organisation may believe it has successfully defended its position but in reality, it may have created an entirely new problem.***

### Part 2: When Defensiveness Creates Complexity

Organisations rarely set out to make simple problems complicated. Usually, complexity emerges from attempts to manage perceived risk.

A question is interpreted as a challenge and a request for clarification becomes something to defend against. Rather than addressing the uncertainty, the organisation concentrates on reinforcing and protecting its position. 

We see politicians do this all the time, defending the response can become more important than answering the question. It's no wonder that we frequently see public trust erode when people feel their questions are being avoided.

***But why should organisations expect a different outcome when they behave in exactly the same way?***

This is closely related to what Chris Argyris described as *organisational defensive routines*: behaviours designed to avoid embarrassment or threat that ultimately prevent organisations from learning.

The irony is that these responses frequently produce the very consequences they are intended to avoid.

An unanswered question invites further questions, repeated reassurances without clarity creates suspicion and informal conversations become formal correspondence, drawing in additional people, processes and resources.

**The organisation begins spending more effort managing the question than it would have spent answering it.**

Each response is treated as a separate procedural obligation... but nobody necessarily stops to consider whether the underlying uncertainty has actually been resolved.

> ***What began as an attempt to minimise risk has become a source of it.***

### Part 3: The Cost of Avoiding Clarity

The solution is rarely another process, policy or carefully worded response. Frequently, it is simply answering the question that was asked.

That does not mean every decision must be justified to everyone's satisfaction and neither does transparency require organisations to disclose everything. But it should recognise that an explanation should address the uncertainty that prompted the question.

Effective leadership requires the confidence to distinguish between scrutiny and hostility. Questions are not necessarily accusations, and clarification is not inherently confrontation.

When organisations fail to make that distinction, they risk turning ordinary conversations into unnecessary disputes.

As a result the consequences extend beyond wasted time and administrative effort. Trust deteriorates, relationships become adversarial and opportunities for learning disappear.

For leaders, the practical lesson is straightforward: **before responding to a difficult question, establish what is actually being asked.** Answer it directly where possible. Where an answer cannot be provided, explain why. And before considering the matter closed, ask whether the original uncertainty has genuinely been resolved.

Because a response is not necessarily an answer, and closing a conversation is not the same as resolving it.

**The real cost isn't always the answer an organisation is reluctant to give. It's everything that happens because the question remains unanswered.**

> ***Sometimes, the most effective way to manage organisational risk is also the simplest: answer the question.***
