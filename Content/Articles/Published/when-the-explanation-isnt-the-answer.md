title: When the Explanation Isn't the Answer

Organisations are remarkably good at explaining their decisions - strategies change, operating models evolve and priorities shift. Sometimes resources need to be allocated differently. 

These are all familiar explanations, delivered through carefully prepared communications and supported by the language of business necessity and often, they're perfectly reasonable on the face of it.

But... there is a difference between explaining why a decision was necessary and explaining how that decision was reached.

It's a distinction that becomes particularly important when someone starts asking questions. 

### Explaining the outcome isn't explaining the decision

Consider a fairly ordinary organisational statement:

***We've reviewed our operating model and determined that changes are necessary to support future business requirements.***

There's nothing inherently wrong with that explanation. Organisations need to adapt - structures that worked five years ago might no longer be appropriate. Responsibilities change, technology creates new possibilities, and business priorities rarely remain static.

But... suppose someone asks when those future requirements were established, what alternatives were considered, or which parts of the operating model remained open to change during the review.

The answer might simply repeat the original explanation and the changes were necessary to meet evolving business needs.

That's not necessarily untrue but it just doesn't answer the question and this is where organisational communication frequently begins to unravel.

The person asking isn't necessarily disputing the business rationale; they're trying to understand the decisions behind it.

Repeating the rationale doesn't provide that understanding.

### Chronology matters more than organisations realise

Most organisations pay considerable attention to documenting decisions - they record approvals, establish governance processes and produce business cases. They can usually identify when a proposal was formally approved or when a change was announced, but those dates don't always tell the whole story.

**When was the decision effectively made?**

That's a rather different question - in complex organisations, decisions rarely happen at a single identifiable moment.

A conversation becomes a proposal, a proposal becomes an assumption and an assumption begins influencing other decisions. 

Responsibilities gradually move, resources are reassigned and people start working towards a future arrangement that hasn't formally been approved.

Eventually, someone signs off the change; on paper, that's when the decision was made.

In practice, much of the organisation may already have adapted to it.

This isn't necessarily evidence of poor governance or improper behaviour - informal planning is an unavoidable part of running an organisation. However, it does create a problem when formal processes are intended to evaluate options or invite meaningful input.

**If the organisation has already begun implementing an outcome, how genuinely open is it to alternatives?**

Sometimes the answer is that substantial flexibility remains... but sometimes it doesn't. The important thing is being able to demonstrate the difference.

### The problem with decisions that emerge gradually

One of the more interesting characteristics of organisational complexity is that responsibility becomes increasingly difficult to locate.

Ask who made a particular decision, and you might discover that nobody quite did: 

- One department identified a requirement
- Another adjusted its resources, a regional team changed its priorities
- Someone revised a job description
- A leadership meeting acknowledged the direction of travel

Each action made sense individually but collectively, they produced a significant organisational change... worryingly, no single person necessarily considered the cumulative consequences.

This is particularly common in large, geographically distributed organisations, where responsibilities overlap and decisions are influenced by multiple reporting structures.

By the time the formal decision arrives, the organisation may have already developed considerable momentum towards a particular outcome.

Changing direction becomes increasingly difficult, not because alternatives have been formally rejected, but because too many other decisions now depend on the emerging arrangement.

The organisation hasn't necessarily decided against the alternatives - it has simply made them progressively harder to pursue.

That's a subtle yet important distinction and it also raises an uncomfortable question about accountability.

If a decision emerges through dozens of individually reasonable actions, who is responsible for examining whether the overall outcome remains appropriate?

### Reassurance is not the same as clarity

When organisational decisions are challenged, the instinctive response is often reassurance.

- The appropriate processes were followed.
- The decision was carefully considered.
- All relevant factors were taken into account.
- We are satisfied that the outcome was reasonable.

These statements may all be entirely accurate, however, they describe the organisation's confidence in its decision rather than the evidence supporting that confidence but confidence isn't always what the person asking the question needs.

- Sometimes they want to understand the sequence of events.
- Sometimes they want to know what alternatives were genuinely available.
- Sometimes they're trying to reconcile two pieces of information that appear inconsistent.

Responding with reassurance can inadvertently make the situation worse - the organisation believes it has provided an answer but the person asking believes their question has been avoided (because it has).

Both become frustrated; the organisation interprets further questions as resistance to an explanation it considers sufficient.

The individual interprets repeated reassurance as an unwillingness to engage with the facts.

What began as a straightforward request for clarification becomes an unnecessary conflict but not because either party necessarily disagrees about the outcome, but because they're having two different conversations.

### A process can be followed without achieving its purpose

Organisations love processes; processes create consistency, establish accountability and provide evidence that appropriate steps have been taken. At least, that's the intention...

But there's a difference between completing a process and achieving the purpose for which it exists. A consultation process, for example, isn't valuable simply because meetings took place and documents were produced.

Its value depends on whether the consultation could meaningfully influence the decisions being considered, likewise, a governance review isn't effective merely because the appropriate committee approved a proposal.

It should also provide an opportunity to challenge assumptions, evaluate alternatives and identify risks.

- A project assurance process that cannot influence a project is little more than reporting.
- A consultation that cannot influence an outcome risks becoming an exercise in communication rather than participation.
- A decision-making process that cannot accommodate new information isn't really evaluating a decision - it's documenting one.

The presence of a process doesn't necessarily demonstrate the presence of meaningful scrutiny and this doesn't mean every consultation must change the outcome or every review must reject a proposal.

Sometimes the original decision is the right one but the process should be capable of producing a different result if the evidence justifies it, otherwise, what exactly is it there for?

### When questions become the problem

There's another organisational behaviour worth examining... sometimes the person asking for clarification gradually becomes the focus of attention rather than the question itself.

- A request for evidence becomes a challenge to authority.
- A question about chronology becomes an unwillingness to accept a decision.
- An attempt to understand the reasoning becomes resistance to change.

This can happen without anyone deliberately trying to avoid accountability. People become invested in decisions they've supported, leaders feel responsible for defending their teams, HR departments focus on demonstrating procedural compliance and project managers want to protect delivery commitments.

These are understandable responses but they can create a culture in which defending a decision becomes more important than examining it... which can be dangerous.

A well-made decision should be able to withstand reasonable questions about how it was reached, it shouldn't require everyone affected by it to accept the explanation without further examination but nor should asking for clarification automatically make someone difficult.

There is, of course, a distinction between legitimate scrutiny and repeatedly challenging a decision simply because the outcome is unwelcome.

Organisations aren't obliged to revisit every decision indefinitely but that distinction is best established through evidence and clear reasoning, not assumptions about the motives of the person asking.

**Four questions that reveal more than another governance framework**

If an organisation wants to understand whether its decision-making processes are genuinely effective, it doesn't necessarily need another committee, approval stage or reporting template.

It might simply need to answer four questions.

1. What was actually decided?
Not the broad strategic intention or the carefully worded announcement.
What specific decision was made, and what did it change?

2. When was it effectively decided?
Not merely when formal approval was recorded.
When did the organisation begin acting on the assumption that a particular outcome would happen?
Were responsibilities, resources or priorities already being adjusted?

3. Who could genuinely influence the outcome?
Who had the authority to challenge the assumptions, propose alternatives or change the direction?
Were those people involved while meaningful options remained available?

4. What evidence demonstrates that alternatives were considered?
Not simply a statement that alternatives were reviewed.
What options existed? Why were they rejected? What information influenced the final decision?
These aren't particularly complicated questions.

But answering them can reveal a surprising amount about how an organisation actually operates and, importantly, the answers might demonstrate that the original decision was entirely reasonable... but that's the point.

Scrutiny isn't about proving that a decision was wrong; it's about establishing why the decision was right, or recognising where it wasn't.

### The difference between being right and being able to explain why

An organisation can make the right decision for the wrong reasons, follow every procedure and still overlook the assumptions that matter most.

Organisational maturity isn't about never making questionable decisions; it's about being willing to examine them, however, organisations often invest more effort in defending outcomes than explaining how they were reached. 

Business cases, governance documents and carefully worded communications can demonstrate that a process was followed without establishing whether it achieved anything meaningful and questions aren't necessarily objections... sometimes they're simply requests for clarity.

A decision can be legitimate without being good, a process can be followed without being useful and an explanation can be accurate without answering the question.

If an organisation cannot explain how it arrived at a decision, perhaps the problem isn't the person asking the question.
