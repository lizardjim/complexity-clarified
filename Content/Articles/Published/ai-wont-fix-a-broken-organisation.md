Artificial intelligence is rapidly becoming the answer to almost every organisational question.

How do we improve productivity?

AI.

How do we make better use of our data?

AI.

How do we reduce costs, automate processes, improve customer experience
or help people make better decisions?

Increasingly, the answer is AI.

And there are good reasons for the excitement. The capabilities
developing around generative AI, automation and autonomous agents are
extraordinary.

But there is a problem...

**AI operates through the organisation that already exists.**

If decision-making is unclear, AI doesn't create accountability. If
knowledge is fragmented, AI can surface conflicting versions of the
truth faster.

If teams operate in silos, connecting them to increasingly sophisticated
technology doesn't suddenly make them collaborate.

And if a process shouldn't exist in the first place, automating it
simply creates a faster unnecessary process.

So perhaps before asking:

***Where can we use AI?***

We should ask a different question:

***What kind of organisation are we about to amplify?***

## Efficiency isn't the same as capability

Imagine an organisation where a relatively straightforward decision
requires approval from seven people.

Someone proposes using AI to improve the process.

AI gathers the information required for the decision. It summarises the
evidence, prepares the documentation and routes the request to each
approver. It automatically chases people who haven't responded and once
everyone has approved it, the system creates a perfect audit trail.

The results look impressive, processing time falls and the
administrative effort disappears. People spend less time chasing
approvals.

The AI implementation is declared a success... but there's a question
nobody asked.

***Why did seven people need to approve it in the first place?***

The organisation has become considerably more efficient at executing a
process that may never have needed to exist.

That's the distinction between **efficiency and capability**:

Technology can help an organisation do what it already does faster.
**Transformation should change what the organisation is capable of
doing.**

Those aren't necessarily the same thing.

## AI can automate bureaucracy

This becomes particularly important as organisations move beyond
individual AI tools towards automation and autonomous agents.

For years, many digital transformation programmes have digitised
existing processes rather than questioning them.

The paper form became the digital form, the spreadsheet became the
dashboard, the manual approval became the automated workflow.

The underlying organisational logic remained largely untouched. AI
creates the possibility of taking this much further.

But that also creates a risk: a bureaucratic organisation with AI can
become **an extremely efficient bureaucracy**.

A siloed organisation with AI can create **more sophisticated silos**.

An organisation with poor information can generate **bad decisions
considerably faster**.

An organisation afraid to make decisions can acquire extraordinarily
sophisticated information with which to continue avoiding them.

AI doesn't automatically remove organisational dysfunction: ***it gives
it leverage.***

## But perhaps that's also the opportunity

There is another way of looking at this, however.

AI might become one of the most useful organisational diagnostic tools a
business has ever had, because trying to automate work forces us to
confront questions organisations have often avoided.

Who actually owns this process?

Why does this approval exist?

Where does this information come from?

Why are three teams maintaining different versions of the same data?

Who is allowed to make this decision?

Why does somebody manually copy information from one system into
another?

What happens when two systems disagree?

Perhaps, most importantly:

**Why do we do this at all?**

These aren't really technology questions - they're questions about how
the organisation works.

The technology simply makes them harder to ignore.

## Technology reveals the organisation

This is why I increasingly think the most interesting thing about AI
isn't simply what it can automate - ***it's what it reveals.***

Every attempt to introduce AI into an organisation encounters the
structures, behaviours, incentives and decisions that already exist.

Sometimes those structures are effective, sometimes they exist for good
reasons and sometimes... nobody can quite remember why they're there.

AI exposes all of it.

That means the friction encountered during AI adoption shouldn't
necessarily be treated as something standing in the way of
transformation.

***The friction may be telling us where the transformation actually
needs to happen.***

If an AI system can't determine who owns a decision, perhaps the problem
isn't the AI.

If an agent can't reliably execute a process because nobody agrees how
the process works, perhaps we've learned something important.

If an AI assistant can't answer a question because organisational
knowledge is fragmented across dozens of systems and teams, perhaps the
technology has revealed a problem that was already there.

## So don't fix the organisation first

None of this means organisations should wait until everything is
perfectly designed before adopting AI.

They'd be waiting forever. Organisations are messy - they change and
evolve, processes accumulate, responsibilities overlap, technology
changes and people find workarounds - that's normal.

The opportunity is to use AI implementation as a reason to question some
of those assumptions.

Before automating a workflow, ask whether the workflow should exist.

Before introducing AI decision support, clarify who actually owns the
decision.

Before connecting organisational knowledge, understand where authority
and truth reside.

Before deploying autonomous agents, decide what autonomy really means.

Before celebrating improvements in productivity, ask what new
organisational capability has actually been created.

Because the most successful organisations won't simply bolt AI onto the
way they already work.

They'll use it as an opportunity to rethink ***how work gets done in the
first place.***

AI won't fix a broken organisation.

***But it might finally make the cracks impossible to ignore.***
